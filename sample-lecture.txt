[00:00] Good morning everyone. Today we are going to talk about photosynthesis, which is the process by which green plants, algae, and some bacteria convert light energy into chemical energy. It is the foundation of nearly all life on Earth, because almost every food chain starts with a photosynthetic organism.
[00:28] Let us start with the big picture. Photosynthesis takes place in the chloroplast, which is an organelle found in plant cells. Inside the chloroplast there are stacks of membranes called thylakoids, and the fluid around them is called the stroma. The overall equation is simple to write: six molecules of carbon dioxide plus six molecules of water, using light energy, produce one molecule of glucose and six molecules of oxygen.
[01:05] The process happens in two main stages. The first stage is the light-dependent reactions, which take place on the thylakoid membranes. Here, chlorophyll in the photosystems absorbs light, mostly in the blue and red parts of the spectrum. The energy splits water molecules, releasing oxygen as a byproduct, and the cell stores the captured energy in two carrier molecules, ATP and NADPH.
[01:42] The second stage is the Calvin cycle, also called the light-independent reactions, which takes place in the stroma. The Calvin cycle uses the ATP and NADPH from the first stage to fix carbon dioxide into sugar. The key enzyme here is rubisco, which is one of the most abundant proteins on the planet. Rubisco attaches carbon dioxide to a five-carbon sugar called RuBP, and after a series of steps the cycle produces G3P, which the plant uses to build glucose and other carbohydrates.
[02:25] Now, a common question is why plants look green. The answer is that chlorophyll absorbs blue and red light very well but reflects green light, so green is the colour that reaches our eyes. That is also why grow lights for indoor plants are often tuned toward blue and red wavelengths.
[02:50] Another point worth remembering is that photosynthesis is not perfectly efficient. Under bright light, a leaf typically converts only about three to six percent of the incoming light energy into chemical energy. Much of the rest is lost as heat, or is used in processes like photorespiration, where rubisco accidentally grabs oxygen instead of carbon dioxide. Plants that live in hot, dry climates, like maize and sugarcane, use a variation called C4 photosynthesis to reduce this waste.
[03:30] Finally, let us connect this to the carbon cycle. Every year, photosynthesis removes roughly one hundred gigatonnes of carbon from the atmosphere. When plants are burned or decay, much of that carbon returns to the air. So forests are not just habitats, they are active carbon stores, and losing them shifts the balance of the whole cycle.
[03:58] To sum up: light energy is captured by chlorophyll in the thylakoids, stored as ATP and NADPH, and then spent in the Calvin cycle to turn carbon dioxide into sugar, with oxygen released along the way. If you remember the two stages, the roles of ATP and NADPH, and the job of rubisco, you have the core of the topic. Next lecture we will look at cellular respiration, which is essentially this process run in reverse.
